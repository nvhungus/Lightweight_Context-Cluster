\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A classifier deployed on a phone or a microcontroller is usually limited by
how much memory its weights occupy, not by how many multiplications the
processor can retire. Storage, over-the-air update size and the resident
footprint of a model all scale with its parameter count, which is why the
lightweight vision literature reports parameters first. That makes a specific
question the operative one: at a fixed parameter budget, which design extracts
the most accuracy? This paper answers it for low-resolution inputs by holding
parameters fixed, training every model under the same budget, and measuring
cost on real hardware instead of inferring it from operation counts.

The distinction between parameters and operations deserves to be made
precisely. A model
shipped to a device occupies flash permanently, travels over the air on every
update, and is resident in RAM for as long as it is loaded; all three scale
with the parameter count and none with the operation count. Arithmetic, by
contrast, is spent only while an inference is running, and on hardware with
idle compute it is close to free. A phone that runs a classifier a few times a
second has arithmetic to spare and a fixed app-size budget it cannot exceed.
This is why the lightweight literature quotes parameters first, and why a
comparison at matched parameters is the one that reflects the constraint that
actually binds. We hold to that framing throughout, and we are explicit about
what it costs us: at a matched parameter budget our design spends more
arithmetic than the baseline it beats.

Context Cluster \cite{coc} offers an unusual answer. Instead of attention or
convolution it mixes tokens by clustering them, and it reports competitive
accuracy at 5.3M parameters on $224\times224$ inputs. Its authors are explicit
that the goal is a new perspective on visual representation rather than state
of the art. What remains untested is whether the mechanism survives
compression to one to three million parameters and $32\times32$ inputs. One
might expect it to struggle there because each stage has few points to group.
We test that expectation directly rather than assuming it: at $32\times32$ the
default configuration groups sixteen points per centre at every stage, and the
ablations vary this from one to two hundred and fifty-six points per centre
without a monotonic effect on accuracy. The failure we document is therefore
not starvation of the clustering operation; it is a failure to repay
parameters at an occupancy that is not degenerate. We treat this as an open
empirical question about operating regime, not as a criticism of the original
work.

The question is under-tested for a structural reason. New token mixers are
normally validated at the scale where they are proposed, and the lightweight
regime is reached afterwards by shrinking a published design rather than by
re-running the comparison that justified it. Shrinking changes the quantities
a clustering mixer depends on. At $224\times224$ a stage-1 feature map holds
thousands of points and a cluster can be populated generously; at $32\times32$
with a stride-1 stem it holds $1024$, and by stage~4 only $16$, at which point
a single centre covers the entire map and the operation is global pooling by
another name. Depth compounds the effect: a lightweight model has a handful of
blocks, so a mixer that needs several layers to propagate information across
an image never gets them. Whether these shifts are fatal or merely
inconvenient is not something one can read off the original paper, which is
the gap this study fills.

To answer it we build a hybrid block in which each stage splits its channels
between a clustering branch and a local convolutional branch in ratio $\rho$,
and then vary one decision at a time: $\rho$, the fold partitioning, channel
shuffle, the stem stride, cluster occupancy, and the choice of local operator.
Every configuration trains for the same number of epochs on the same recipe,
repeats over three seeds, and is compared both with a Welch test on the seed
means and with an exact paired McNemar test on the $10{,}000$ test
predictions. Where a configuration has fewer than three seeds we say so in the
table.

The ablations point one way. Removing the local branch while holding the stem,
folds, depth and width fixed --- the comparison that isolates clustering from
everything it is usually bundled with --- costs $0.39$ pp. Removing the
clustering branch instead gains $0.94$ pp, at 10\% fewer parameters. Fold
partitioning and channel shuffle change accuracy by less than run-to-run
variation. The largest single effect in the whole study is the stem stride, at
$-5.59$ pp, and because stride~2 halves the resolution entering every
subsequent stage, most of that belongs to resolution rather than to anything
specific to clustering. At this scale the local branch carries the accuracy
and the clustering branch is a net cost.

Following that conclusion produces a design with no clustering in it at all.
Scaled to 2.71M parameters, given $7\times7$ depthwise kernels, and trained
with logit distillation, it reaches $61.30 \pm 0.39$ on Tiny-ImageNet. It
leads a parameter-matched ShuffleNetV2-x1.5 \cite{shufflenetv2} by $1.51$ pp
and its own ResNet-18 teacher by $2.59$ pp, both significant over seeds and
confirmed by a paired test on every seed individually. We chose
ShuffleNetV2-x1.5 as the primary baseline because an earlier version of our
model lost to it, and we report it rather than substituting something weaker.

Two caveats belong in the opening rather than buried later. The win holds
under distillation; with plain cross-entropy the margin over
ShuffleNetV2-x1.5 is a smaller but still significant $0.90$ pp on
Tiny-ImageNet and a non-significant $0.39$ pp on CIFAR-10, and the model
merely ties its teacher. And the accuracy is bought with compute: at
matched parameters the proposed model performs 231M multiply--accumulates
against ShuffleNetV2-x1.5's 96M, though still well under the 557M of the
ResNet-18 it beats.

This paper contributes a controlled ablation of context clustering in the
lightweight low-resolution regime and the negative conclusion that it does not
pay for itself there; an all-local configuration that beats a
parameter-matched baseline and its own teacher on two datasets; a cost
protocol using graph-verified latency, per-batch-size memory reset and
weights reported separately from activations, together with the observation
that realized arithmetic throughput varies by nearly tenfold across the models
compared, so operation counts are a poor proxy for measured latency; and
a release of every configuration, seed, per-image prediction and provenance
record behind the numbers.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Lightweight convolutional networks.} MobileNetV2 \cite{mobilenetv2}
established the inverted residual with a linear bottleneck, and ShuffleNetV2
\cite{shufflenetv2} argued that operation counts are a poor proxy for latency,
proposing four design rules derived from measured speed instead. Its channel
split, depthwise convolution and channel shuffle give a strong
accuracy-per-parameter trade-off, and the x1.5 width multiplier lands close to
the budget we study, which is why we adopt it as the primary baseline.
ConvNeXt \cite{convnext} later showed that widening depthwise kernels from
$3\times3$ to $7\times7$ buys roughly $0.7$ pp on ImageNet at negligible cost,
and RepLKNet \cite{replknet} pushed the idea further and attributed the gain
to effective receptive field rather than capacity. Our final design uses both
observations.

Two of ShuffleNetV2's four guidelines bear directly on what we measure:
fragmentation into many small operations reduces parallelism, and element-wise
operations are not negligible. Its own block --- channel split, depthwise
convolution, concatenation, shuffle --- is exactly that pattern, and we find
$87.5\%$ of its eager batch-one latency is kernel-launch overhead against
$77.1\%$ for our model. Graph capture removes the penalty and reverses the
ordering, which is why we report both execution modes.

\textbf{Token mixers.} MetaFormer \cite{metaformer} separated the transformer
block into a token mixer and a channel MLP and showed that the surrounding
structure matters more than the mixer itself; replacing attention with average
pooling cost surprisingly little. That claim has a consequence this paper
leans on. If the skeleton carries most of the performance, then any comparison
between two mixers is only meaningful when the skeleton either side of them is
held identical, and a reported gap between two published networks with
different stems, depths and widths tells us almost nothing about their mixers.
It also raises the possibility that a mixer validated inside one skeleton
contributes little inside another, which is what we test here. Context Cluster
\cite{coc} substitutes a clustering operation into that slot: points are
assigned to centres by similarity, aggregated, and dispatched back, with fold
partitioning limiting assignment to local tiles. Reported results are at 5.3M
parameters and $224\times224$. Holding the rest of the skeleton fixed is what
lets a single number be attributed to clustering rather than to the
architecture around it.

\textbf{Knowledge distillation.} Hinton et al. \cite{hinton} match a student
to a teacher's softened logits, and the technique is routine in lightweight
vision because it closes part of the gap to a larger model at no inference
cost. Decoupled knowledge distillation \cite{dkd} separates the target and
non-target components of that loss and reweights them. A recurring difficulty
in comparisons is that distillation is often applied only to the proposed
model; we apply the identical recipe, teacher and seed to every student in
this paper, and report cross-entropy results alongside.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{The hybrid block and its design space}

Every model in this study is built from the same four-stage MetaFormer
skeleton \cite{metaformer}. Write $X \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times H \times W}$ for
the input to a block. The block is

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:block1}
Y = X + \gamma_1 \odot \mathcal{D}\big(\mathcal{T}(\mathrm{BN}(X))\big),
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:block2}
Z = Y + \gamma_2 \odot \mathcal{D}\big(\mathcal{M}(\mathrm{BN}(Y))\big),
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{T}$ is the token mixer, $\mathcal{M}$ a channel MLP of
expansion ratio~3, $\mathcal{D}$ stochastic depth \cite{stochasticdepth}, and
$\gamma_1, \gamma_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{C}$ are LayerScale \cite{layerscale}
vectors initialised at $10^{-5}$ and broadcast over space. Only $\mathcal{T}$ differs between the
models we compare; everything else in \eqref{eq:block1}--\eqref{eq:block2} is
held fixed, as are the stage depths $[1,1,2,1]$ and the stride-2 point
reducers that separate stages.

The hybrid block parameterises the token mixer by a local ratio
$\rho \in [0,1]$. Splitting the channels as
$C_\ell = \lfloor \rho C \rceil$ and $C_c = C - C_\ell$,

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mixer}
\mathcal{T}(X) = W_f \ast \big[\, \mathcal{C}(X_{1:C_c}) \;\Vert\;
\mathcal{L}(X_{C_c+1:C}) \,\big],
\end{equation}

with $\Vert$ concatenation along channels, $\mathcal{C}$ the clustering
branch, $\mathcal{L}$ the local branch, and $W_f$ a $1\times1$ fusion
convolution that degenerates to the identity when either branch is empty; an
optional channel shuffle precedes the split. The
clustering branch follows \cite{coc}: the feature map is divided into fold
tiles, centres are proposed within each tile, points are assigned to centres
by cosine similarity, aggregated, and dispatched back. Occupancy $r$, the
number of points per centre, is set by the proposal grid. Setting $\rho = 0$
recovers a pure clustering model and $\rho = 1$ removes clustering entirely,
which is what makes $\rho$ the variable the study turns on. Section~\ref{sec:ablation}
reports each of these knobs varied in isolation.

\subsection{The All-Local configuration}

The configuration the ablations select contains no clustering and no
hand-designed filters. Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows it.

Two channels beyond the image carry each pixel's normalised $x$ and $y$
coordinate in $[-0.5, 0.5]$, so the stem sees five input channels rather than
three. We inherit this from the hybrid model, where position entered the
clustering similarity, and hold it fixed across every configuration compared.
We did not ablate it and claim no benefit from it; we note it because it is
the one respect in which the all-local model is not a plain convolutional
network.

The stem is a $3\times3$ convolution at stride~1, so stage~1 operates at the
full $32\times32$ resolution. This is the single most consequential choice in
the model: moving the stem to stride~2 costs $5.59$ pp
(Table~\ref{tab:ablation}), far more than any other decision we varied. At
$32\times32$ input there is little spatial information to discard, and
discarding half of it at the first layer is not recoverable downstream.

A stride-2 stem does two things at once, and the earlier version of this work
credited the wrong one. It halves the resolution reaching every stage, and it
also reduces the number of points available to each cluster. The effect
therefore decomposes into an occupancy component and a resolution component,
and our ablations bound the first. A stride-2 stem reduces the stage-1 map to
$16\times16$, which at the default fold and proposal sizes yields four points
per centre --- the same occupancy as the $r = 4$ ablation, which costs $1.36$
pp. Since $r = 4$ holds resolution fixed at $32\times32$ and varies only
occupancy, this bounds the occupancy contribution at roughly a quarter of the
$5.59$ pp stem effect under an additivity assumption; the remaining
${\approx}4.2$ pp reflects resolution and its interaction with occupancy. The
practical reading is that the stem stride is not evidence for
clustering: it is evidence that $32\times32$ inputs cannot afford early
downsampling, which would be true of any architecture.

Stage~1 mixes tokens with a learned $3\times3$ convolution. The hybrid model
used fixed filters whose weights encode a sparse local binary pattern and
which expand the channel count eightfold before a pointwise projection;
Section~\ref{sec:ablation} shows that a plain learned convolution matches them
in accuracy at equal parameters while using $2.50\times$ less activation
memory, so we use the learned convolution.

The accounting behind that swap is worth stating, because the first version of
this work reported it incorrectly. A fixed-filter branch applies a depthwise
convolution that expands $C$ channels to $8C$, normalises, and projects back
with a $1\times1$ convolution, costing $72C$ frozen weights, $16C$
normalisation parameters and $8C^2 + 2C$ in the projection, or $8C^2 + 90C$ in
total. A learned $3\times3$ convolution with batch normalisation costs
$9C^2 + 2C$. The difference is $C(88 - C)$, so at the stage-1 width $C = 78$
of the final model the fixed-filter branch is the larger of the two, by $780$
parameters or $0.03\%$ of the network. The frozen weights are still weights and the
expansion still performs multiply--accumulates, so the branch is not free on
either axis, and the eightfold intermediate is what drives its activation
memory.

Stages~2 to~4 mix tokens with a $7\times7$ depthwise convolution followed by a
$1\times1$ pointwise convolution,

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:local}
\mathcal{L}(X) = \sigma\big(\mathrm{BN}(W_p \ast
  \sigma(\mathrm{BN}(W_d \circledast X)))\big),
\end{equation}

where $\circledast$ is depthwise convolution, $\ast$ is dense convolution and
$\sigma$ is GELU. Separating the kernel this way costs
$C_\text{in}k^2 + C_\text{in}C_\text{out}$ parameters against
$C_\text{in}C_\text{out}k^2$ for a dense kernel, a ratio of
$1/C_\text{out} + 1/k^2$; at $k = 7$ and the widths used here the pointwise
term dominates, which is why enlarging $k$ is nearly free. The wide kernel
follows \cite{convnext}: with only five blocks in the entire network, depth
cannot grow the effective receptive field, so each layer has to reach further.
Widening from $3\times3$ to $7\times7$ adds 35{,}600 parameters, $1.3\%$ of
the model, and 2.5M operations, $1.1\%$.

Channel widths are $[78, 116, 232, 310]$ with depths $[1, 1, 2, 1]$, giving
2{,}707{,}416 parameters with a 200-class head. The widths were chosen to
match ShuffleNetV2-x1.5 at 2{,}683{,}624 parameters so that the headline
comparison is at an equal budget; no other hyperparameter was tuned to the
baseline. A final normalisation, global average pooling and a linear
classifier complete the network.
