\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

The result we are most confident in is negative. Context clustering, inserted
into a fixed MetaFormer skeleton and compared against a convolutional branch
at the same parameter count, loses by $1.03$ pp at one to three million
parameters and $32\times32$ input. The supporting machinery built around it
--- fold partitioning, channel shuffle, a tuned occupancy --- moves accuracy
by less than seed variance, and the one setting that matters a great deal,
the stem stride, matters because of resolution rather than because of
clustering. None of this contradicts \cite{coc}, which reports a different
operating point; it bounds where the mechanism is useful.

The positive result is narrower than the headline number suggests, in three
specific ways.

\textbf{It depends on distillation.} Under cross-entropy the proposed model
leads ShuffleNetV2-x1.5 by $0.47$ pp, which does not reach significance over
three seeds ($p = 0.051$), and it does not beat the ResNet-18 teacher at all
($-0.07$ pp, $p = 0.405$). Only with distillation does the margin reach $1.49$
pp and the teacher comparison reverse. Every claim in this paper about
exceeding the teacher is therefore a claim about the distilled setting.

\textbf{We cannot explain the distillation asymmetry.} Given an identical
teacher, temperature and loss weight, the proposed model gains $2.52$ pp from
distillation while ShuffleNetV2-x1.5 gains $1.50$ pp and ShuffleNetV2-x1.0
gains $1.58$ pp. The obvious explanation, that a weaker model has more room to
improve, predicts the opposite of what we observe: the proposed model has the
higher cross-entropy accuracy and still gains more.

Three candidate mechanisms are consistent with what we see and we can
separate none of them with the runs we have. The proposed model may be more
capacity-limited than parameter count suggests, so that the denser supervision
in a softened label distribution buys it more than the one-hot signal does;
this would predict that the gap narrows as the training budget grows, which we
have not varied. The $7\times7$ depthwise kernels give it a wider effective
receptive field than the baseline at the same depth, and a teacher's logits
encode inter-class structure that may be easier to absorb with a wider spatial
view; this would predict that the $3\times3$ variant of the same model gains
less, which we did not measure because we ran only the final kernel size under
distillation. Or the effect may be a property of width rather than
architecture, in which case scaling the baseline further would reproduce it.
Distinguishing these needs a grid over kernel size, width and training budget
with distillation on and off --- roughly four times the compute this study
used. We report the asymmetry because it is what produces the headline margin
and a reader is entitled to know the margin depends on it; we decline to
attribute a mechanism without the experiment that would test one. A reader who
wants the architecture-only comparison should read the cross-entropy column,
where the two models are within half a point of each other.

\textbf{The accuracy is paid for in compute.} At matched parameters the
proposed model performs $2.41\times$ the operations of ShuffleNetV2-x1.5,
occupies $3.12\times$ its peak memory at batch 128, and reaches $0.66\times$
its throughput. It is cheaper than the teacher it beats on every static axis
--- $4.16\times$ fewer parameters, $2.41\times$ fewer operations, $2.60\times$
less memory at batch one --- but against the parameter-matched baseline the
exchange is accuracy for compute.

Where that arithmetic goes is not mysterious. A stride-1 stem keeps stage~1 at
$32\times32$, so the first stage alone processes $1024$ positions at $78$
channels, four times as many positions as a stride-2 design would, and the
$7\times7$ depthwise kernels at later stages multiply the per-position cost
again. The operations are the price of the two decisions the ablations
selected, and they cannot be removed without giving back the accuracy those
decisions bought. We also measured that the price is smaller in wall-clock
terms than in operation counts: against the baseline the arithmetic ratio is
$2.41\times$ but the graph-captured batch-one latency ratio is $1.07\times$,
because the baseline's fragmented block spends most of its own batch-one time
on kernel launches rather than arithmetic.

This gives a usable decision rule rather than a blanket recommendation. A
deployment bound by storage, update size or resident footprint --- a mobile
application with a fixed size budget, a device with limited flash --- should
prefer the proposed model, which is more accurate at the same parameter count
and barely more expensive at batch one. A deployment bound by arithmetic or by
batched throughput --- a server classifying large batches, a device whose
accelerator is already saturated --- should prefer ShuffleNetV2-x1.5, which
gives up a point and a half of accuracy for a third of the operations and half
again the throughput. We state the rule in both directions because the
parameter axis is the one we optimised and it would be misleading to present a
model that loses on throughput as uniformly better.

Two further limits constrain how far the numbers travel. Both datasets are
evaluated at $32\times32$, which for Tiny-ImageNet means downsampling from the
native $64\times64$; our absolute accuracies are therefore not comparable to
published results at the native resolution, and we make no such comparison.
And while every Tiny-ImageNet configuration is run with three seeds, the
CIFAR-10 results come from a single seed each; their significance rests on
paired tests over $10{,}000$ test images within that seed, which is a weaker
guarantee than seed-level replication.

Finally, a point about what the negative result does and does not cover. We
tested one clustering formulation --- similarity-based assignment to proposed
centres with fold-limited scope, aggregation and dispatch, as specified in
\cite{coc} --- inside one skeleton, at one resolution, across a narrow band of
parameter budgets. We did not test clustering as a class of token mixer.
Soft or learned assignment, centres shared across stages, a different
similarity, or an occupancy schedule tuned jointly with the stem could each
behave differently, and nothing here rules them out. The claim we are entitled
to is narrower than ``clustering does not work at small scale'': it is that
this clustering operation, inserted in place of a convolutional branch at
equal parameters, does not repay its cost in this regime. We think that
narrower claim is still worth reporting, because it is the configuration a
practitioner would reach for first when shrinking \cite{coc}, and because the
comparison that would otherwise be cited in its favour confounds the mixer
with the stem, the depth and the fold design.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We set out to test whether context clustering earns its place in a lightweight
classifier at low resolution, and found that it does not: at a matched
parameter budget, replacing the clustering branch with a convolutional one
improves Tiny-ImageNet accuracy by $1.03$ pp while using fewer parameters, and
the design choices that surround clustering contribute less than seed
variance. Carrying that conclusion through yields a clustering-free model
which, at 2.71M parameters with $7\times7$ depthwise kernels and logit
distillation, reaches $61.21 \pm 0.40$ on Tiny-ImageNet and $96.68$ on
CIFAR-10, exceeding a parameter-matched ShuffleNetV2-x1.5 by $1.49$ pp and its
own ResNet-18 teacher by $2.46$ pp while using $4.16\times$ fewer parameters
than that teacher. The gain is conditional on distillation and costs
$2.41\times$ the operations of the parameter-matched baseline, and we report
both alongside the measured memory and latency rather than parameter counts
alone. The ablations, configurations, seeds and per-image predictions behind
every number are released.
